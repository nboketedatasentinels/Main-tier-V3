The case · read first
SARS Modernisation, 2007-2018 -- and the four-year silence
Collapse
Background

In 2007, the South African Revenue Service (SARS) launched a programme called Modernisation -- a multi-year, in-house digitisation effort to move the country's tax collection system from a largely paper-based operation to an automated digital platform. The programme was led by Barry Hore, then SARS Chief Operating Officer. By 2014, Modernisation had transformed SARS into one of the most respected tax authorities on the African continent. Compliance rates had risen, e-filing had become standard, and the programme was on track to complete by 2015.

On 27 September 2014, Tom Moyane was appointed Commissioner of SARS. On 12 December 2014 -- three months into his tenure -- a newsflash appeared on the internal SARS communication system announcing that the Modernisation programme had been scrapped. There was no consultation with the senior IT leadership team. Hore had not been informed. SARS official Linda Burger, testifying later, described the moment in these terms: more than 66 million rand of in-flight projects were placed at immediate risk; over 22 million rand in assets captured in the e-Central system were subsequently never accounted for; and, in her words:

"It was just like the curtain had fallen."

In July 2015, Moyane signed a contract worth more than 200 million rand with the global advisory firm Gartner to review and replace the IT strategy. The contract was awarded through a closed, non-competitive tender that did not meet National Treasury procurement regulations. A separate Bain and Company contract for business model restructuring cost a further 164 million rand. Hore resigned from SARS shortly after Moyane's arrival. He was not consulted on the halt of his own programme.

In 2018, after Moyane's suspension, retired Judge Robert Nugent was appointed by President Cyril Ramaphosa to head a Commission of Inquiry into Tax Administration and Governance at SARS. Hore returned to give public testimony. So did Andre Rabie, Andre Scheepers, Linda Burger, and other senior IT executives who had remained at SARS through the dismantling. Their testimony described, in granular detail, what they had seen, what they had not been asked, and -- crucially -- what they had not surfaced to anyone with the authority to act on it. The Nugent final report, delivered in December 2018, found that the cost to SARS to fix what had been dismantled was approximately 1 billion rand. In 2023, the United States Securities and Exchange Commission separately announced a settlement with Gartner under the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act in connection with the SARS contract.

The dynamic to analyse

This case is not about state capture. It is about senior practitioners who watched a working transformation programme being dismantled in real time, were not consulted, and did not surface their objections to the right authority at the right time. They surfaced them four years later, to a judicial commission, when the cost was already 1 billion rand.

The questions below ask you to examine the discipline of escalating risk before being asked, and what it would have taken to interrupt this trajectory in 2014 rather than testify about it in 2018.

Question 01
Outcome 3 · Scoping discipline

Two scope decisions Hore should have locked down in October 2014

Imagine you are Barry Hore in October 2014, one month into Moyane's tenure. You can already see the direction of travel. Name two scope decisions about the Modernisation programme you would have insisted on locking down at that moment to protect the work that had been done. Justify each in one sentence.
Two specific decisions, each named in governance language and justified with reference to the SARS situation. The test is whether the decisions you name could have been actioned in October 2014 -- not whether they would have ultimately succeeded against political pressure. "Better planning" or "stronger governance" are gestures, not scope decisions. A scope decision names what gets locked, who locks it, and what the lock prevents.

Question 02
Outcome 4 · Known risk vs managed risk

What does this case show about known risk vs managed risk?

Burger, Rabie, and Scheepers all testified that they were not consulted before the 12 December 2014 announcement. What does this case tell you about the difference between a risk being known by senior practitioners and a risk being managed? Use the case to explain the distinction.
The distinction is structural -- not a difference of awareness, but a difference of escalation discipline. The case shows what known-but-not-managed looks like in practice. The argument has to use the case as evidence: who knew, who did not act, what action would have constituted "managing" the risk rather than just knowing it.

Question 03
Outcome 5 · Senior reporting language

Draft the opening two sentences of the briefing note

Draft the opening two sentences of the briefing note you would have written -- in November 2014, before the announcement -- to escalate to the SARS Board, the Auditor-General, or the Minister of Finance. Lead with structured risk. Name the specific transformation cost at stake. Make the ask explicit.
Two actual sentences, written as a briefing note opens -- not a description of what the briefing would say. Pick one escalation target (Board, Auditor-General, or Minister of Finance) and write to that specific recipient. Risk leads in sentence one. The ask is in sentence two. The recipient should be able to act on the briefing as written, not need to ask for a follow-up to understand what's being requested.

Question 04
Outcome 5 · Healthy disagreement vs political behaviour

Healthy disagreement vs political behaviour -- with one specific example

Several SARS executives stayed silent at the time and testified four years later. Distinguish 'healthy disagreement' from 'political behaviour' in the context of a senior practitioner watching a working programme being dismantled by a new boss. Use one specific example from a programme you are working on or have worked on -- you do not need to name the organisation.
This is the highest-weighted question. The distinction is structural -- they look similar from the outside, they are different in what they protect. The example you choose has to actually contain the dynamic -- a moment where you could see the boundary between healthy challenge and political positioning, and could have gone either way. A generic disagreement story without that boundary is not what the question is asking for.
