The Conversation You've Been Avoiding

Pick the conversation you have been avoiding -- the one with a member of your team that has been deferred for more than two weeks because the AI rollout has made it harder to have. Run it. Then come back and reflect on what actually happened. Five stages: who, your opening line verbatim, what you heard, what shifted in the room, what is now in motion. Reflection is the practical -- the conversation is the cost of admission.

What good looks like
Aim for these as you go.
  • The person is named -- by initial or short reference -- and the reason this conversation has been avoided is honest, not staged.
  • The opening line is recorded verbatim -- the actual words you used, not the version you wish you had used.
  • What you heard is the unexpected part -- the moment the conversation turned, the comment that was not in your script.
  • What shifted is observable -- a sentence the other person said that they would not have said before, a posture change, a question they asked.
  • What is now in motion is one specific thing -- a meeting booked, a decision unblocked, a permission granted -- with a date.
1
Step 1
Set Up the Conversation Before You Reflect

Before the five-stage reflection, two pieces of context. Who the conversation was with -- by initial or short reference. And why it had been avoided -- the honest reason, not the version that lets you off the hook. "I was busy" is not a reason. "I did not know how to raise the AI rollout's effect on her seniority without sounding like I was managing her out" is a reason.

01
Who and When
Person -- by initial or short reference. Their role. The date the conversation actually happened. Whether you initiated or they did.
02
Why It Had Been Avoided
The honest reason. Not "I was busy". The structural reason that was actually keeping the conversation off the calendar.
2
Step 2
Reflect Across the Five Stages

Walk the conversation in five stages. The order matters -- this is the anatomy of what happened, not a thematic essay. Stage 2 is the one that gets cleaned up most often in reflection. Resist that. Write the opening line as it actually came out of your mouth, not as you wish it had.

The Conversation Anatomy
Five stages · in order · written from inside the conversation, not above it
01
Who Was in the Room
The person -- and a sentence on what you knew about their state going in. What you were carrying about them, accurately or not, when you opened the door.
02
Your Opening Line, Verbatim
Exact words. The version you actually said, not the version you rehearsed in your head. If you are tempted to clean it up, that is the line you most need to write down.
03
What You Actually Heard
The part that was not in your script. The moment the conversation turned -- a comment, a silence that landed differently than expected, the thing she said that you had not predicted she would say.
04
What Shifted in the Room
Observable. A sentence she said that she would not have said before. A posture change. A question she asked that she had not asked in months.
05
What Is Now in Motion
One specific thing. A meeting booked, a decision unblocked, a permission granted, a process changed. With a date. Not "we agreed to keep talking".
3
Step 3
Name the Cost of the Avoidance

The conversation cost something to have. The avoidance cost something more. Two checks. First: what specifically cost more by being deferred six weeks (or however long) than it would have cost on day one? Second: what is the structural pattern this conversation belongs to -- the kind of conversation you tend to defer? Naming the pattern is what makes this Practical generalise. Without it, you have done one conversation, not changed an instinct.

01
The Cost of the Delay
What specifically got more expensive by being deferred. Not "trust eroded". An observable cost.
02
The Pattern
The kind of conversation you tend to defer. Not "hard ones". A specific structural pattern.
AI-Powered Activity

Use AI to Pressure-Test the Reflection

Conversation reflections fail at two places every time -- the practitioner cleans up the opening line in retrospect, and what shifted gets written as how the practitioner felt rather than what was observable. Both make the reflection unmarkable and unrepeatable. AI as an outside reader catches both. Use it before you submit.

1
Test Whether This Was a Real Conversation
Ask AI to test whether the reflection reads as the actual conversation that happened -- or whether it has been cleaned up into the conversation you wish you had had.
Paste this prompt
I am reflecting on a hard conversation I had with someone on my team during an AI rollout. The five stages are: (1) who was in the room: [paste]. (2) opening line verbatim: [paste]. (3) what I heard: [paste]. (4) what shifted: [paste]. (5) what is now in motion: [paste]. Pressure-test this. Specifically: (1) Does the opening line sound like something that came out of a real mouth in a real moment, or has it been polished into something I wish I had said? (2) Does what I "heard" name a specific moment, comment, or silence -- or has it been summarised into a thematic statement that any reflective leader could write? (3) Does "what shifted" describe an observable change -- a sentence the other person said, a posture, a question -- or have I described how I felt the room had shifted, which is not the same thing? Tell me where the reflection has gone abstract and rewrite the weakest stage with operational specificity.
Where did the reflection go abstract? What is the rewritten version?
2
Test What Actually Shifted vs What Stayed the Same
Ask AI to test whether what is now "in motion" is genuinely new -- a meeting that would not have been booked, a decision that would not have been made -- or whether it would have happened anyway and the conversation is being credited with it.
Paste this prompt
What I claim is now in motion after the conversation is: [paste]. Pressure-test on three criteria. (1) Of the things now in motion, which would have happened anyway in the next two weeks even without the conversation? Strip those out. (2) Of what remains, which has a date attached -- a specific meeting booked, a decision made, a written commitment with a deadline? (3) What did NOT shift that I had hoped would shift -- and naming that is part of the reflection, not a failure of it. The honest version of "what is now in motion" is shorter than the optimistic version. Help me find it.
What is genuinely new vs what would have happened anyway? What did not shift?
AI Lens
Six months from now, when the AI rollout has shifted again, which conversation are you most likely to defer for the same structural reason -- absence of a frame, awkwardness about the rollout's effect on someone's authority, the wait for a better version of the question? What is the trigger that should override the wait next time?
Take This to Peer-to-Peer

Before you submit, talk it through.

Conversation reflections fall apart at the same place every time -- the practitioner cleans up the opening line and writes "what shifted" as how they felt rather than what was observable. A peer who has done their own reflection can hear both at the same speed an assessor will. Bring this to your peer-to-peer call this week.

What to bring

Read the opening line aloud first. If your peer's first reaction is "people actually talk like that?" -- the line is genuine. If their first reaction is "that sounds prepared" -- it has been cleaned up in retrospect, and the rest of the reflection is at risk of having been cleaned up too. Then read what shifted. If your peer can describe the moment back to you in concrete terms -- the question N asked, the offer she made -- the observation is real. If they cannot, what is on the page is feeling-language pretending to be observation.

Three questions for your peer to ask you
  • The opening line -- does it sound like something a real mouth said in a real moment, or like the version you wish you had used?
  • What shifted -- can you describe a specific moment, sentence, or question, or have you written feeling-language pretending to be observation?
  • The pattern you named -- if you ran into the same structural setup tomorrow with someone else, would naming the pattern actually change what you do, or is it self-aware language without a behaviour change attached?
