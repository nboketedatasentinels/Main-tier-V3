Five Pillars Self-Audit

Score your team on the five pillars of heart-centred leadership against observable behaviour in the past 30 days. Not the team you wish you had. The one that turned up. Then name the lowest pillar -- the one you would rather not name -- and the cost it has been producing in your AI-era work specifically.

What good looks like
Aim for these as you go.
  • Each pillar scored against observable behaviour from the past 30 days, not gut feel.
  • The lowest pillar is the genuinely lowest one -- the one that is uncomfortable to name.
  • The cost is concrete: a decision delayed, a person disengaged, a meeting derailed. Not "team morale".
  • The AI-era context is explicit -- rollout pressure, role redesign, technical authority shift, change fatigue compounding -- not generic team stress.
1
Step 1
Frame the Team You Are Scoring

Before you score, name the team. Not the org chart team -- the actual delivery team running through the AI-era work right now. The one whose behaviour you have observed in the past 30 days. Specificity here makes the audit honest.

01
The Team
Name the team -- size, function, your relationship to it. Two sentences.
02
The 30-Day Window
What real situations from the past 30 days will you score against? Two or three brief markers.
2
Step 2
Score the Five Pillars

Score each pillar 1 to 5 against what you have actually observed in the past 30 days. For each one, write the specific behaviour that earned the score -- the meeting, the comment, the moment. Score against evidence, not against the version of the team you want to defend in this audit.

The Five Pillars Scorecard
1 = rarely visible · 3 = sometimes visible · 5 = consistently observable in the past 30 days

Look at the scorecard. The lowest pillar is your starting point for the Blueprint in Week 2. AI-Powered Activity

Use AI to Pressure-Test the Audit

Self-audits fail at the same two places every time -- you pick the safer pillar, and the cost statement slides into generic team language. AI as an outside reader can hear both faults faster than a peer can. Use it before you take this Practical to peer-to-peer.

1
Test Whether You Picked the Genuinely Lowest Pillar
Ask AI to test whether the pillar you named is the genuinely lowest one -- or the one that is safest to talk about. Most practitioners pick the second.
Paste this prompt
I am running a self-audit on my team across five pillars of heart-centred leadership: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skill. My scores against observable behaviour in the past 30 days were: [paste your five scores]. The evidence I wrote against each was: [paste your evidence sentences]. The pillar I have named as lowest is: [paste your lowest pillar]. Pressure-test this choice. Specifically: (1) Is the pillar I have named the genuinely lowest one, or the safest one to name -- the one that is more about the team than about me as the leader? (2) Looking at my evidence, is there a different pillar where the evidence is actually weaker than I am admitting? (3) If a peer who knows my team independently scored these pillars, where would they most likely disagree with me?
What did AI flag? What will you change about your lowest pillar choice -- if anything?
2
Stress-Test the Cost Statement
Ask AI to test whether the cost statement is concrete and AI-era specific -- or whether it has slipped into generic team language. If the cost statement is generic, the Blueprint built from it next week will be generic too.
Paste this prompt
My cost statement for the lowest pillar in the team's AI-era work is: [paste your cost statement]. Pressure-test it on three criteria. (1) Is it concrete -- a specific decision delayed, person disengaged, or meeting derailed -- or does it stay at the level of "team morale" or "team stress"? (2) Does it explicitly name an AI-era dynamic: rollout pressure, role redesign, technical authority shift, or change fatigue compounding? (3) Could a different practitioner reading this cost statement understand what is at stake in my team specifically, or could it apply to any team in any organisation? Tell me where the statement is generic, and rewrite the weakest sentence with operational specificity.
Where did the cost statement go generic? What is the tightened version?
AI Lens
Six months from now, when the AI rollout has shifted again -- new tooling, new role definitions, a new pressure cycle -- which of the five pillars is most likely to drop a score, and what early signal would you watch for before it shows up in observable behaviour?
Take This to Peer-to-Peer

Before you submit, talk it through.

Self-audits fall apart at the same place every time -- the pillar named is one the practitioner is comfortable being seen failing at. A peer who has done their own audit can hear when the choice is honest and when it is strategic. Bring this Practical to your peer-to-peer call this week.

What to bring

Read your scorecard out loud, then read the evidence you wrote against each pillar. Watch your peer's face at the lowest-pillar reveal. If they look surprised, you may have picked the safer one. If they nod, you probably named what they would have named. Either way, the conversation is useful before you submit.

Three questions for your peer to ask you
  • Is the lowest pillar the one you would rather not name -- or the one that lets you off the hook because it is more about the team than about you?
  • Is the cost stated in concrete terms -- a decision delayed, a person disengaged, a meeting derailed -- or has it slipped into "team morale" language?
  • Does the AI-era context show up in the cost statement -- rollout pressure, role redesign, technical authority shift, change fatigue compounding -- or could this cost statement apply to any team in any year?
