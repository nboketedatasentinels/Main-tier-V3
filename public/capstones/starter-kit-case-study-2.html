The case · read first
Eastman Kodak, December 1975 -- and the four decades that followed
Collapse
Background

Steven Sasson, an electrical engineer at Eastman Kodak, built the first working digital camera prototype in December 1975. The camera weighed 3.6 kilograms, took 23 seconds to capture a 0.01 megapixel black-and-white image, and stored it on cassette tape. Sasson presented the prototype internally to senior Kodak executives in 1976.

The reception was polite, but the technology was shelved. Sasson has said publicly, including in a 2008 New York Times interview and a 2015 oral history with the Computer History Museum, that the executives' first response was:

"But it's filmless photography. Why would anyone want to look at their pictures on a television set?"

Kodak held the foundational patent on digital photography. The company later licensed the patent to competitors including Canon, Nikon, and Sony, generating an estimated 3 billion US dollars in royalties between 1991 and the patent's expiry in 2007. By the early 2000s, the same competitors had used digital photography to displace Kodak's film business.

Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 19 January 2012. By that point, the company's photographic film business had collapsed from its 1996 peak revenue of approximately 16 billion US dollars to under 1 billion. Sasson left Kodak in 2009. He was awarded the United States National Medal of Technology and Innovation in 2009 by President Obama for inventing the digital camera.

The dynamic to analyse

This case is not a story about a missed technology bet. It is a story about a senior practitioner who saw an opportunity in his own organisation's scope, built a working prototype to demonstrate it, presented it to the people who controlled the decision, and failed to win the room.

The questions below ask you to examine why the pitch did not land, and what an audience-matched proposal would have looked like.

Question 01
Outcome 1 · Opportunity recognition

Was Sasson's the right opportunity to identify, given his scope?

Sasson identified an opportunity. Was it the right opportunity for him to identify, given his scope as an engineer at Kodak in 1975? Justify your answer in 2-3 sentences.
The question is about scope, not about the technology. The opportunity Sasson saw and the opportunity Kodak missed are not necessarily the same thing -- a practitioner's scope is what they can act on, not what the company should have done in hindsight. Be precise about which scope you are answering for.

Question 02
Outcome 1 · Cost of inaction

Estimate the cost of inaction in concrete terms

Estimate the cost of inaction to Kodak in concrete terms. Use figures from the case where useful, but the analysis matters more than the precision.
Concrete means specific -- revenue figures, royalty figures, market share, time-to-irrelevance. The case provides several figures that can be used or extended. The analysis matters more than the precision: a defensible logic with rounded figures beats a precise calculation built on weak reasoning.

Question 03
Outcome 2 · Audience-matched framing

If you had been Sasson, what would you have done differently?

If you had been Sasson in 1976, what would you have done differently in the framing of the pitch? Name the decision-maker, their priority at that moment, and the specific reframe you would have used to win their support.
Three things to name -- the decision-maker (by role at Kodak in 1976), their actual priority at that moment (what they were rewarded for, what they were defending), and a reframe written as a sentence Sasson could have used. "He should have communicated the business case better" is not a reframe; it is a description of communication. The reframe needs to be testable -- a specific sentence with a specific priority embedded in it.

Question 04
Outcomes 1 + 2 · Application to your own scope

Apply the same discipline to an opportunity in your own scope

Identify one opportunity in your own current organisational scope where you risk being the practitioner who saw it but could not get traction on it. Name the opportunity, the decision-maker, and the one thing you would change about how you frame it after analysing this case.
This is the highest-weighted question. The opportunity needs to be specific to your actual work -- not transferred from the case. The decision-maker is named by role (the person who controls the decision, not the person who agrees with you). The one change to framing is concrete -- a specific change you will make, not a general intent to "frame things better".
