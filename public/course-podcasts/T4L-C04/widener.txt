WHAT WILL BE ASSESSED
All three questions come from the crisis stretch saved in this file. Focus on the contrast Hiro
draws between the night before the whale hit -- when he lay awake running through everything
that could go wrong, teeth shaking -- and the moment the boat actually sank, when he says he
was "very, very calm." Pay attention to exactly what he says pulled him out of the fear: the
message from his late uncle, saying "arigato" (thank you) to the boat, and "living now instead
of thinking about the future." Note too that his partner Shinbo was in a panic while Hiro stayed
calm, and that they survived in part because of an emergency bag they had prepared in advance,
which held a GPS and a satellite phone. You do not need the rest of the episode.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** So I know your story as a sailor, but before you did that, you actually were a mountaineer then.

**Hiro Iwamoto:** Yes. Then after that, yes, I can do more and more challenges. And when I sailed one time, the first time, I realized with wind, this sailboat can go very far. So let's try to sail across the Pacific, the biggest ocean in the world. I started having the dream.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** So you're living in Japan, you grew up in Japan right on the Pacific Ocean and it just seemed to you always, that's a massive challenge. Is that how it dawned on you? Just because it was there?

**Hiro Iwamoto:** Yes, that's why, yeah. I was thinking, you know, I grew up surrounded by ocean. Always thinking, what's beyond this ocean? So let's try to sail across the Pacific. That's the biggest challenge, sailing across the Pacific, from Japan to California. I started thinking and I started telling my dream. And I always say: in this world, once you start saying the biggest or big dream, there are two groups. One group called the dream-killer: no, you cannot do that, you cannot see, so stop it, even stop thinking. The other team I call dream supporter: wow, that's an incredible challenge. Let's think together how you can do it.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** And how did you do it? What was your first, you know, big voyage?

**Hiro Iwamoto:** After, I had a lot of good supporters and I tried one time in 2013 with a newscaster, Mr. Shinbo, and we tried leaving from Fukushima because I wanted to encourage the kids there after the tsunami. Because they had resentment and hatred towards the oceans because the tsunami wiped out their parents or beautiful, cute sisters and brothers. So I wanted to send a message: the ocean is still waiting for you, let's go out sailing to be friends with it. So we left there and we sent a message and I did the charity, so that I could send two sailboats, small sailboats to them. And excellent when we left, beautiful weather … Six days after we left, we had a collision with a whale. Seven in the morning, I heard three crashes: boom, boom, boom. After that sound, I felt that was a whale. That's instinct, sixth sense. Because I'm blind, I feel, you know, I have different sense.

**Hiro Iwamoto:** And I thought we had a big crack or damage. So I was listening carefully: there you go, something different, water coming in. So I took the floorboard out and stuck my hand there. There you go. Just as I thought, water was coming in. So I woke him: “Shinbo, boat is sinking!” And he woke up and he started putting the life raft on while I was picking up stuff that should go into the life raft. So after that: “Shinbo, calm down!” Yeah, I was telling that, and we transferred ourselves into the life raft with the emergency bag we prepared. And floated around for 11 hours with the storms, five-metre waves. And luckily we prepared that emergency bag containing the important thing: Handy GPS and satellite phone. So longitude, latitude we are calling and telling the Japanese maritime self-defense force, and that's why they could find us.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** Did the boat just sink?

**Hiro Iwamoto:** Yeah, the boat sank and then the airplane, seaplane came and they're circling. The first airplane couldn't land because the ocean was too rough. They can land within three meters, but they went back because they are running out of fuel. But the captain said, in the evening the sea might be calmed down a little bit, so please send the second one. And the Japanese government decided to send the second one, which circled; they landed and then their rubber boat came. And because we are floating around, so this big ship cannot follow us. So the rubber boat came and picked us up and they left.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** Were you terrified?

**Hiro Iwamoto:** I was very, very calm.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** Why?

**Hiro Iwamoto:** Because at night before the whale hit our boat and the sea was rough. So more and more, you tip sideways and the highest is more than five metres, 15-foot waves. So I started worrying about the future: if these waves break this sailboat, what will happen? If this inflatable life raft doesn’t inflate automatically, what will happen? Even inflated, how can we transfer ourselves? Can we do that? My teeth were shaking, making sound.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** You'd already been thinking about this the day before, but you were calm when it actually happened.

**Hiro Iwamoto:** My late uncle sent a message again. Are you saying arigato, thank you, to Aeolus the sailboat? She is going through this storm to save your life. Say arigato. And I started saying arigato, arigato, arigato. And started living now instead of thinking, thinking, future. Right. That calmed me down. Next day, even after the whale hit a boat, I was calm. That's why I'm telling, I was telling Shinbo, my sailing partner, calm down … he is in a panic, right? So even in the life raft, I will be fine. I was so calm because I was living now. So that message helped me a lot.

**Robin Pomeroy, Meet the Leader:** I mean, the message is useful because panicking and being scared is not going to help you, is it? No, no.

**Hiro Iwamoto:** No, no. Be now - that’s the power word.
